# Oasis Keeper

A desert oasis building and water-management simulation, inspired by
ClanFolk, Dwarf Fortress and RimWorld, but narrowed down to the thing those
games only touch on: **keeping a patch of desert alive with water you have
to find, move, store and shade yourself.** There is no money and no economy
-- the only currency is water and the plants it sustains.

Built with **Godot 4.3 / GDScript**.

## Why Godot + GDScript

Godot gives a 2D scene graph, an editor, and a resource system (`.tres`)
for free, which matters a lot for a data-driven plant catalogue. GDScript,
used with static typing and `Packed*Array` grids instead of one `Node` per
tile, is fast enough for a simulation of this size (~21,600 tiles) as long
as the *data* is laid out flat and the *systems* iterate it in tight loops
-- which is how this project is structured. If it ever needs to scale much
further, every system talks to the world only through `WorldMap`'s flat
arrays and `EventBus` signals, so any one of them (WaterSystem being the
obvious candidate) could be reimplemented as a C++ GDExtension without
touching the rest.

## Running it

Open `project.godot` in Godot 4.3+, or run headless:

```
godot --path OasisKeeper
```

There is a headless smoke test that builds a working water network and
reports what the simulation actually does:

```
godot --headless --path OasisKeeper --quit-after 4200 -- --sim-selftest
```

It finds an aquifer, digs two runs out of the mountain -- one open, one
covered -- plants a date palm, places gates/storage/well/shade, and then
prints the water depth along both channels every so often. A typical tail:

```
SELFTEST t=4200  open    [10.00, 10.00, 8.47, 3.96, 2.15, 2.32, 1.33, end=0.93]
SELFTEST t=4200  covered [10.00, 10.00, 10.00, 10.00, 9.71, 9.81, 9.61, end=9.25]
SELFTEST t=4200  aquifer 5446/6578
```

That is the whole game in three lines: water moves tile to tile and tapers
downstream, an open channel loses most of it to the sun before it arrives,
a covered one delivers nearly all of it, and the aquifer draws down while
you pull on it.

## Controls and UI

The build UI is in two parts:

- A **category bar across the bottom**, always visible: Inspect, Channels,
  Storage & Wells, Structures, Plants, Overlays, Demolish.
- Picking a category opens a **detail panel down the left side** listing
  that category's items with full descriptions. Clicking the same category
  again closes it, so the map is never permanently covered.

Inspect and Demolish are direct tools -- they select immediately and close
the panel.

- **Left click / drag** on the map: apply the selected tool.
- **Right click**: back to Inspect.
- **Number keys**: pick a category (shown on each button). While a category
  is open, **Q/W/E** pick items within it.
- **Arrow keys / WASD**: pan. **Scroll wheel**: zoom.
- The cursor outline turns **green** where the current tool can build and
  **red** where it cannot; the reason appears at the bottom of the panel.
- A tile inspector sits bottom-right and follows the cursor.

## How the water works

This is the part worth understanding before playing.

**Aquifers are the source.** They are organic, irregular bodies of water
inside the mountain rock, generated by a randomized flood fill, each with
its own finite volume and slow recharge. You cannot see them from the
surface -- switch the **Aquifers (geology)** overlay on to find them. They
are drawn cyan when full and shift toward red as they are drained.

**There is no "tap" building.** You reach an aquifer by digging a canal
into the rock until it touches one. Any canal placed on mountain rock
automatically becomes a **Mountain Tunnel** (the cursor tells you this is
about to happen), so you use the same tool the whole way from the mountain
down to your fields. A tunnel tile sitting on an aquifer body draws from it
automatically.

**Water flows tile to tile, downhill.** Every water structure has a floor
below the terrain and holds a depth of water on top of it. Each tick, every
connected pair of neighbours moves a fraction of their hydraulic head
difference (floor + water depth) from the higher to the lower. So water runs
along a channel, pools where the ground flattens, and will not climb a rise.
The valley slopes gently from north to south, giving the whole map a
consistent downhill direction to work with.

**Trenches follow the ground; buried conduits are cut to a gradient.** This
distinction is load-bearing. An open canal is a trench: its floor follows
the surface, so it cannot cross high land. Mountain tunnels and covered
canals are bored/buried on a descending gradient, so their floor is capped
at a datum just above the valley floor -- the qanat principle. Without that,
a channel leaving a range would have to climb the ridge crest and then the
scree apron of the foothills, and water would never reach the valley at all.
On flat valley ground the cap never binds and both behave identically.

You can *see* this: canal tiles show their fill level directly (the stream
widens and deepens in colour as the tile fills, and an empty channel shows
bare bed), and white arrows show which way water is actually moving. Turn
the arrows off in the build panel if you find them noisy.

**The central trade-off is open vs covered.**

| | Open Canal | Covered Canal |
|---|---|---|
| Digging cost | 1x | 2x |
| Evaporation | full, and worse in heat/wind/sun | negligible |
| Waters adjacent soil | yes | no |
| Can cross high ground | no (follows the surface) | yes (cut to a gradient) |
| Looks like | visible water channel | slabbed lid with joint lines |

An open canal is how you actually irrigate -- it wets the ground either
side of it, which is what your plants drink. A covered canal is how you
*transport* water a long way without losing it. A real oasis needs both:
covered for the long haul from the mountains, open where you want the water
to soak in. Mountain tunnels are covered by definition.

Gates let you split the network and decide where the water goes. Storage
comes in open (**Reservoir**, evaporates, shade it with palms) and covered
(**Cistern**, holds more, loses nearly nothing) forms. **Wells** tap rare
shallow groundwater pockets on the valley floor -- use the Groundwater
overlay to find somewhere worth sinking one.

## The oasis effect

Every open water surface and moist soil tile evaporates each tick at a rate
driven by temperature, wind, local humidity and shade -- full shade cuts
evaporation by up to 85%. That water does not vanish: it raises
`air_moisture` on its tile, which diffuses to neighbours and is advected
downwind, so a thriving planted area builds a humid pocket that trails in
the wind's direction and makes further evaporation slower. Plants transpire
into the same layer.

Shade is recomputed every tick from plant canopies (radius and strength
scale with species and growth stage -- date palms cast the largest and
strongest) and from man-made shade structures, which are instant and need
no water but are weaker than a mature palm and yield nothing. That is the
deliberate trade-off for not planting a date palm.

## World generation

Generation runs as ordered geological passes, which is what makes the map
read as a place rather than layered noise:

1. Two meandering ranges (ridged multifractal) with a scree apron.
2. A valley floor between them: a shallow cross-valley basin plus a slope
   down the valley's long axis, so the whole map drains toward one outlet.
3. A **wadi network** traced by steepest descent from the foothills out
   into the valley, then carved -- so the channels branch and meander the
   way real drainage does.
4. **Alluvium** deposited either side of each wadi. This is the fertile
   ground, and plants grow markedly faster on it than on dune sand -- which
   is exactly why real oases sit along the wadis.
5. **Dune fields** on the dry ground far from wadis and ranges.
6. **Aquifers** flood-filled inside the rock.

Terrain types are Dune Sand, Desert Pavement, Alluvium, Scree and Rock,
each with its own fertility. A typical map comes out around 43% dune sand,
16% pavement, 8% alluvium, 13% scree, 20% rock, with 7-9 aquifer bodies.

## Architecture

```
scripts/
  autoload/        Singletons: the simulation itself
    GameConfig.gd      every tuning constant, in one place
    EventBus.gd        global signals; systems never reference each other directly
    GameClock.gd       day/night + 4-season calendar, drives the fixed sim tick
    WorldMap.gd        flat per-tile data layers
    ClimateSystem.gd   wind, temperature, shade, evaporation, transpiration, humidity
    WaterSystem.gd     aquifer tapping, tile-to-tile flow, irrigation
    PlantSystem.gd     plant growth, water draw, health, harvest
    BuildSystem.gd     placement rules, terrain auto-morphing, construction queue
  world/
    Tiles.gd           shared Terrain/Structure enums (see note below)
    MapGenerator.gd    the geological passes above
  entities/PlantInstance.gd    lightweight runtime state for one specimen
  resources/PlantData.gd       data-driven species definition (Resource)
  rendering/
    TerrainBaker.gd          bakes the terrain image + tiling grain texture
    WorldRenderer.gd         data overlays + structures + plants + flow arrows
    NightCycleController.gd  day/night tint
  camera/CameraController.gd
  ui/  HUD.gd, BuildMenu.gd, TileInspector.gd   built in code, no .tscn layouts
       UILayout.gd    explicit anchor/offset helpers (see note below)
  MainController.gd   thin coordinator: input -> tool -> system calls
  SelfTest.gd         headless smoke test
  Screenshotter.gd    headless screenshot + Control-rect dump (dev only)

data/plants/*.tres    14 species as data files
```

Systems never touch rendering and never hold references to UI. They either
read/write `WorldMap`'s arrays directly (the hot path) or communicate via
`EventBus` signals (the cold path). `WaterSystem` does not know
`PlantSystem` exists except through `WorldMap.soil_moisture`.

> **Note on `Tiles.gd`:** the `Terrain` and `Structure` enums live on a
> plain class rather than on the `WorldMap` autoload, because an autoload is
> a runtime Node instance -- `WorldMap.Structure.GATE` is not a constant
> expression and cannot be used inside a `const` dictionary.
> `WorldMap` re-exports them (`const Structure = Tiles.Structure`) so
> existing call sites keep working.

### Adding a new plant

Drop a `PlantData` `.tres` into `data/plants/`. No code changes --
`PlantSystem` scans the directory at startup and the build menu populates
itself, grouped by category, with a generated description. Copy an existing
file and adjust; see `scripts/resources/PlantData.gd` for what each field
does.

### Adding a new building type

1. Add an entry to `Tiles.Structure`.
2. Add its placement rule to `BuildSystem.can_place()`, a failure message
   to `placement_hint()`, and a dig time to `dig_ticks_for()`.
3. If it should auto-adapt to terrain, handle it in `resolve_structure()`.
4. Add behaviour to `WaterSystem.simulate_tick()`.
5. Add drawing to `WorldRenderer._draw_structures()`.
6. Add it to `BuildMenu.CATEGORIES` and `TOOL_STRUCTURE`, and route it in
   `MainController._apply_tool_at_mouse()`.

### Tuning

Every constant controlling flow speed, evaporation, aquifer size and
recharge, shade falloff, temperature curves and drought tolerance lives in
`scripts/autoload/GameConfig.gd`, each with a comment on the intuition
behind it. Nothing else hardcodes a tuning number.

## Rendering approach

- Static terrain is baked once into an image at 4 sub-pixels per tile with
  hillshading and per-material grain, so the ground has texture rather than
  reading as flat blocks at 24px tiles.
- Soil moisture, shade and the data overlays are 1px-per-tile images
  stretched with **linear** filtering, so they read as smooth fields.
- Structures, plants and flow arrows are drawn with the immediate-mode
  draw API and **culled to the visible viewport**, so canals can be drawn
  as genuinely connected channels (each tile draws arms toward its
  connected neighbours) with a visible water level, at any map size.
- Plants are drawn procedurally per category -- palms get radiating fronds,
  shrubs get overlapping clusters, flowers get petals -- scaled by growth
  and browned by poor health.

## Development tools

Two things exist purely to make this project verifiable without a human at
the keyboard, which is how it was built and tuned:

- `--sim-selftest` (see above) builds a working network and prints water
  depths along it.
- `--screenshot <path>` renders a frame and writes a PNG, then dumps the
  on-screen rect of every Control. Optional companions:
  `--screenshot-frames N`, `--screenshot-zoom Z`, `--screenshot-hour H`
  (freezes the clock at that hour so lighting is reproducible), and
  `--screenshot-open <category>` to open a build category. Run it under a
  virtual display:

```
xvfb-run -a godot --path OasisKeeper --rendering-driver opengl3 \
  --resolution 1600x900 --quit-after 600 \
  -- --screenshot /tmp/shot.png --screenshot-frames 400 --screenshot-hour 12
```

The Control-rect dump is worth knowing about: it is what caught a bug where
`Control.set_anchors_preset()` (whose default `keep_offsets = false`
recalculates offsets to *preserve the control's current rect*) faithfully
preserved the 0x0 size a panel had in the scene file, so the entire build
menu existed, reported `visible = true`, and drew nothing. `UILayout` now
sets anchors and offsets explicitly to remove that trap.

## Known simplifications

- Shade is a canopy/structure coverage model, not real-time sun-angle
  shadow casting. Deliberate: you can see exactly what is shading a tile
  without reasoning about time of day.
- Water flow is a mass-conserving cellular automaton, not a fluid solver.
- Construction is instant labour-free digging on a timer; there are no
  workers to schedule yet.
- Art is entirely procedural. `WorldRenderer` and `TerrainBaker` are the
  only files an art pass would need to touch.
